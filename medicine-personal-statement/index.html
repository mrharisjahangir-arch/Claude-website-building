The UCAS statement now has three questions

Since 2026 entry, UCAS asks three separate questions instead of one essay. You have 4,000 characters across all three, each answer needs at least 350 characters, and the questions themselves do not count towards the limit.

You can split the characters however you like. For medicine, most students give the most space to question 3, because that is where work experience and reflection live.

The UCAS deadline for medicine is 15 October 2026 at 6pm for 2027 entry.

The three questions

  • Why do you want to study this course or subject?
  • How have your qualifications and studies helped you to prepare for this course or subject?
  • What else have you done to prepare outside of education, and why are these experiences useful?

How to answer each question

Question 1

Why medicine?

  • Start with a specific moment, not “I have always wanted to”
  • Show a realistic picture: the pressure, the uncertainty, the teamwork
  • Explain why doctor rather than another health role
  • Keep it about you, not a famous doctor or TV show
Question 2

Your studies

  • Link a subject to a skill: biology to physiology, maths to data
  • Mention an EPQ, a book or a lecture, and what you took from it
  • Show how you study: independent learning, handling setbacks
  • Do not repeat your grades. UCAS already has them
Question 3

Beyond the classroom

  • Work experience, volunteering and part-time jobs all count
  • Pick two or three experiences and go deep, not ten briefly
  • For each one: what you did, what you noticed, what you learnt
  • Caring for a relative or a shop job can reflect as well as a hospital

The reflection method

What? So what? Now what?

  • What? Describe the moment in one sentence. “A patient on the ward asked the same question three times.”
  • So what? Say what you noticed and why it mattered. “The doctor answered calmly each time, and the patient relaxed.”
  • Now what? Say what you learnt or changed. “Patience is part of communication. I now check people have understood before I move on.”

Where to get experience

Admissions tutors care more about what you learnt than where you went. These are all real routes.

Hospices and hospitals

  • Pilgrims Hospices in Canterbury, Ashford and Thanet takes volunteers from 16
  • East Kent Hospitals runs a volunteering programme for 16 to 18 year olds

First aid and youth groups

  • St John Ambulance cadets from age 10 to 17
  • Scouts, Guides and Duke of Edinburgh for teamwork and leadership

Online work experience

  • RCGP’s Observe GP, free for UK students aged 16 and over
  • BSMS virtual work experience, a free online course

Everyday roles that count

  • Part-time jobs dealing with customers
  • Tutoring or coaching younger students
  • Caring for a family member

Common personal statement mistakes

  • Listing experiences→Reflect on two or three in depth
  • “I want to help people”→A specific moment that shows it
  • One essay chopped into three→Answer each question directly
  • Heavy medical jargon→Plain, clear English
  • Starting in October→Draft over the summer, redraft in September
