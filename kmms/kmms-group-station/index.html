The KMMS group station

Previous KMMS MMIs included a 40-minute group station where each candidate is assessed individually. This guide covers what assessors watch for, how to plan the 40 minutes, the skills to build and how to practise them.

Written by Haris, KMMS medical student · Updated September 2026

KMMS MMI mock interview →Back to the KMMS guide

Short answer

Previous KMMS MMIs included a 40-minute group station.

A handful of candidates work on a task together while you are each assessed as an individual. It is not about talking the most. It is about helping the group reach a good answer.

KMMS does not guarantee the same format for 2027, so follow your official invitation. For the full picture, see our KMMS Medicine 2027 guide.

What assessors are watching

An assessor can only mark what they see and hear. Make your good qualities visible.

Contribution

Quality over volume

  • One clear idea with a reason beats five half ideas
  • Speak early so you are part of the discussion
  • If you have a good idea, say it
Listening

Build on others

  • Use people’s names when you respond
  • Refer back to what someone said earlier
  • Ask a follow-up question before disagreeing
Inclusion

Notice who is quiet

  • Invite them in with a specific question
  • Give credit when their idea is used
  • Slow the group down if someone is lost
Process

Keep the group on track

  • Suggest a plan in the first minutes
  • Offer time checks
  • Summarise decisions out loud
Composure

Handle disagreement well

  • Disagree with the idea, not the person
  • Change your mind openly when persuaded
  • Stay calm if someone dominates
Judgement

Keep the goal in view

  • Re-read the brief if the group drifts
  • Weigh options against the task
  • Think about fairness and safety

How to use the 40 minutes

The exact task will vary. This rough shape works for most group tasks.

  1. First 5 minutes

    Understand and plan

    • Read the brief carefully
    • Check the goal: “So we need to decide…?”
    • Suggest a rough plan and time checks
  2. Next 25 minutes

    Explore options

    • Share ideas and the reasons behind them
    • Weigh each option against the goal
    • Bring in anyone who has not spoken
  3. Last 10 minutes

    Decide and summarise

    • Start narrowing down in good time
    • Summarise what you agreed and why
    • Name any disagreement fairly

The soft skills behind a strong group station

These take months to build, not a night. Start now and they will help in every MMI station, not just the group one.

Speaking up on the spot

Why it matters

There is no preparation time in a group task. If you freeze, the moment passes and the assessor sees nothing.

Build it
  • Join a debate club or Model United Nations
  • Answer first in class discussions
  • Read out loud to family or younger children
Practise it

Pick a news headline, set a 60-second timer and explain it out loud. Record it and listen back once.

Active listening

Why it matters

The best contributions build on what others have said. Listening is what makes your ideas relevant.

Build it
  • Volunteering where you mostly listen, such as a hospice or care home
  • A part-time job with customers
  • Befriending or mentoring younger students
Practise it

In your next conversation, sum up what the other person said before you reply: “So you’re saying…”.

Explaining your reasoning

Why it matters

Assessors mark why you think something, not just what you think. A reason turns an opinion into a contribution.

Build it
  • Essay subjects and an EPQ
  • Debating both sides of a motion
  • Explaining homework to a friend
Practise it

Answer everyday questions with a point, a reason and an example, out loud, in under 30 seconds.

Leading without dominating

Why it matters

Medicine is teamwork. Helping the group work well shows maturity that talking the most never can.

Build it
  • Sports captaincy or coaching
  • Duke of Edinburgh expeditions
  • Scouts, Guides or St John Ambulance cadets
Practise it

In your next group project, be the person who keeps time and summarises rather than the one who talks most.

Handling disagreement

Why it matters

Respectful challenge keeps patients safe. Assessors want to see you disagree without damaging the group.

Build it
  • Debating
  • Student council or committee work
  • Customer-facing work where people complain
Practise it

Use the pattern: “I see why you’d say that. My worry is… What if we…?”

Staying calm under pressure

Why it matters

One clumsy moment in 40 minutes will not sink you. Panicking about it might.

Build it
  • Public speaking or school assemblies
  • Music exams, drama or sport
  • First aid training
Practise it

Learn box breathing (in 4, hold 4, out 4, hold 4) and use it before every practice task.

How the skills work together

Each skill makes the others easier.

  • Listening feeds speaking. When you really listen, you always have something relevant to add, so speaking up gets easier.
  • Confidence makes room for others. It takes confidence to pause the group and ask a quiet person what they think.
  • Calm lets you change your mind. If you are not anxious about “winning”, you can say “that’s a better idea than mine” and look stronger for it.
  • Reasoning makes disagreement safe. Disagreeing with a clear reason sounds thoughtful, not rude.

Phrases that help

Use your own words. These show the kind of thing that moves a group forward.

  • “Shall we spend a minute agreeing what a good answer looks like?”
  • “Building on what Priya said…”
  • “We haven’t heard from you yet. What do you think?”
  • “I see it a bit differently, and here’s why…”
  • “Can we check we all agree before we move on?”
  • “We’ve got ten minutes left. Shall we start narrowing down?”
  • “Let me try to summarise where we’ve got to.”
  • “That’s a better idea than mine. Let’s go with it.”

Common group station mistakes

  • Talking the most→Fewer, better contributions
  • Staying silent to be safe→One clear idea early on
  • Declaring yourself leader→Offer a plan instead of a title
  • Arguing to win→Aim for the best group decision
  • Ignoring the brief→Re-read the goal when the group drifts
  • Forgetting the clock→Offer time checks and start narrowing down
  • Repeating others’ ideas as your own→Credit them by name

How to practise before the day

Run a mock group task

Get three or four friends together. Pick any topic you can discuss, set a 20-minute timer and aim to agree one decision as a group.

Add an observer

Rotate one person out to watch. They count interruptions, invitations and summaries, then give feedback.

Practise with strangers

Real groups are people you have never met. School societies and online study groups help you get used to that.

Watch yourself back

With everyone’s permission, record a practice. Notice how often you listen, speak and bring others in.

Get real team experience

Volunteering, sport and Duke of Edinburgh give you teamwork stories for other stations and for your personal statement.
